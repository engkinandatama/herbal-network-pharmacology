# Comprehensive Phytochemical Profiling and Therapeutic Evaluation of *Phaleria macrocarpa* (Mahkota Dewa)

## In the Management of Metabolic Syndrome and Diabetic Nephropathy

---

## 1. Introduction

The medicinal plant *Phaleria macrocarpa* (Scheff.) Boerl., widely known in Southeast Asia as Mahkota Dewa or "God's Crown," has garnered extensive scientific attention due to its multifaceted therapeutic potential, particularly in addressing the global burden of metabolic diseases.[1, 2] As a member of the Thymelaeaceae family, this evergreen tree is endemic to Papua, Indonesia, but has been successfully cultivated across the Indonesian archipelago and Malaysia for its pharmacological properties.[3, 4]

The plant's utility in traditional medicine—ranging from the treatment of diabetes mellitus to cancer, hypertension, and renal disorders—is now being substantiated by rigorous phytochemical investigations using advanced analytical platforms such as LC-MS/MS, UPLC-Q-ToF, and high-field NMR spectroscopy.[1, 5, 6, 7]

The primary challenge in modern pharmacognosy regarding *Phaleria macrocarpa* involves the isolation and standardization of its complex secondary metabolites, which include distinctive benzophenones, xanthones, flavonoids, and lignans.[1, 8] Current research emphasizes that the biological efficacy of the plant is not the result of a single compound but rather a synergistic interplay of several bioactive markers that target various pathological pathways.[9, 10, 11]

Of particular significance is the plant's role in mitigating the complications of chronic hyperglycemia, specifically diabetic nephropathy, which is characterized by renal oxidative stress, glomerular hypertrophy, and inflammatory damage.[6, 12, 13]

## 3. Xanthones and Benzophenones: The Core Bioactives

The most distinctive class of compounds in *P. macrocarpa* are the xanthones and benzophenones, which often exist as glycosides. **Mangiferin** (C₁₉H₁₈O₁₁), a C-glucosylxanthone, is a significant marker found in high concentrations in the seeds and leaves.[1, 2, 10] Its importance in diabetes management stems from its dual ability to inhibit α-glucosidase and act as a potent iron chelator, thereby mitigating iron-overload-induced oxidative damage in the liver and heart, which are common comorbidities in diabetic patients.[1, 10, 20]

Benzophenones such as **Phalerin** and **Mahkoside A** serve as chemotaxonomic markers for the species.[1, 2, 3] Phalerin, originally isolated from the leaves and later found in the fruit, is a benzophenone glycoside identified as 3,4,5-trihydroxy-4-methoxybenzophenone-3-O-β-D-glucoside.[2, 3, 7] Studies have demonstrated that Phalerin exhibits a pro-apoptotic effect by regulating B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) protein family expression and activating caspase-9 pathways, which is particularly relevant in controlling aberrant cell growth and providing protection against renal cell proliferation in nephropathy models.[2, 19]

**Mahkoside A**, identified as 4,4'-dihydroxy-6-methoxybenzophenone-2-O-β-D-glucopyranoside, has been isolated from the pit and bark, providing a basis for the plant's cytotoxic properties.[1, 2, 24]

Another critical benzophenone is **2,6,4'-trihydroxy-4-methoxybenzophenone**, which has been isolated from both the leaves and fruit.[9, 16, 18] This compound has shown potential in managing obesity-related diabetes by significantly inhibiting pancreatic lipase activity, the enzyme responsible for dietary fat absorption.[16, 18] Furthermore, it induces neurite outgrowth in PC-12 cells at a concentration of 50 μg/mL, suggesting a secondary neuroprotective role that could be explored for treating diabetic neuropathy.[16, 18]

## 4. Flavonoid Diversity and Distribution

Flavonoids are ubiquitously distributed throughout *P. macrocarpa*, with the fruit pericarp being the most concentrated source, yielding total flavonoid levels as high as 161.3 mg rutin equivalent/g DW.[4, 14]

The presence of Vitexin, Isovitexin, Apigenin, and Luteolin in *P. macrocarpa* has been documented in phytochemical surveys of plants used by indigenous communities like the Toraja, where Mahkota Dewa is valued for its broad pharmacological activities.[23]

**Luteolin** and Vitexin are particularly noted for their higher binding affinities toward α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes compared to their isomers, providing a molecular basis for the plant's efficacy in reducing postprandial glucose levels.[26, 29, 30]

Isoflavones such as Glycitin and intermediate precursors like Naringenin chalcone have also been identified, indicating a complex biosynthetic pathway that results in a highly antioxidant-rich matrix.[10, 22, 31]

## 5. Lignans and Sterols: Lipophilic Bioactives

The lignan profile of *P. macrocarpa* includes **Pinoresinol**, **Lariciresinol**, and **Matairesinol**, isolated and confirmed through chiral column analysis.[7, 19] These compounds are not only significant for their antioxidant capacity but also for their ability to normalize blood pressure in fructose-induced hypertensive models by inhibiting Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme (ACE).[20]

Pinoresinol has further been investigated for its in vitro anti-inflammatory properties through the modulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, which is a key driver of diabetic complications.[32]

Phytosterols, specifically **β-Sitosterol**, are present in the seeds and fruit flesh.[21, 26] Research utilizing supercritical carbon dioxide (subCO2) extraction has revealed that these lipophilic compounds play a more significant role in the inhibition of α-glucosidase than previously estimated, often outperforming hydrophilic polyphenols in specific non-polar extraction environments.[21]

## 6. Mechanisms of Antidiabetic Action

The antihyperglycemic efficacy of *Phaleria macrocarpa* is achieved through a multi-targeted approach involving the inhibition of carbohydrate-digesting enzymes, the enhancement of insulin sensitivity, and the protection of pancreatic β-cells.[6, 33]

### 6.1 Inhibition of α-Glucosidase and Carbohydrate Digestion

The most well-documented antidiabetic mechanism of *P. macrocarpa* is the inhibition of α-glucosidase, an enzyme located in the brush border of the small intestine that catalyzes the final step of carbohydrate digestion.[6, 33, 34] Methanolic extracts of the fruit pericarp have demonstrated a **58.33% reduction in blood glucose levels** in diabetic rat models compared to pre-treatment values.[6]

Molecular dynamics simulations and network pharmacology have identified specific compounds responsible for this activity. **Swertianin** and 8-C-glucopyranosyleriodictylol have shown significant stability and strong protein-ligand interactions over 100 ns simulations, confirming their potential as natural alternatives to pharmaceutical inhibitors like acarbose.[5, 21]

The inhibition of α-amylase by compounds like Vitexin further complements this by reducing the initial breakdown of starch into maltose.[26, 30]

### 6.2 Insulin Mimicry and Pancreatic Restoration

Extracts of *P. macrocarpa* exhibit insulin-mimetic properties by enhancing glucose uptake in peripheral tissues.[35] In vitro models using HepG2 (hepatocyte) cells showed that both aqueous and methanol-chloroform extracts could increase glucose uptake by up to **5-fold**, a performance level comparable to metformin.[35]

In vivo, the administration of *P. macrocarpa* leaf extract at a dose of 500 mg/kg BW has been found to restore the architecture of the Islets of Langerhans in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats.[33] This restorative effect includes an increase in the number of islet cells and the repair of damaged β-cells, which are crucial for maintaining endogenous insulin secretion.[2, 33]

## 7. Diabetic Nephropathy and Nephroprotective Effects

Persistent hyperglycemia leads to the development of diabetic nephropathy (DN), a condition marked by glomerular basement membrane thickening, mesangial expansion, and eventual renal failure.[12, 13, 36] The nephroprotective activity of *Phaleria macrocarpa* is primarily attributed to its ability to modulate key growth factors and suppress oxidative stress in renal tissues.[6, 10, 12]

### 7.1 Suppression of VEGF and TGF-β Expression

Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) and Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β) play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of DN.

### Table 3: Comparison of Treatment Effects on DN Markers

| Parameter | Metformin Treatment | *P. macrocarpa* Treatment | Significance |
|-----------|---------------------|---------------------------|--------------|
| VEGF Expression | Moderate reduction | Significant reduction (lower than Metformin) | p<0.01 |
| TGF-β Expression | Moderate reduction | Significant reduction (Methanolic extract) | p<0.01 |
| BUN Levels | Moderate decrease | Significant decrease | p<0.05 |

**References:** [6, 10, 12, 13]

Results from immunohistochemical studies indicate that methanolic and aqueous extracts of *P. macrocarpa* pericarp significantly reduce the expression of both VEGF and TGF-β in the glomeruli of alloxan-induced diabetic rats.[12] This intervention successfully arrests glomerular growth and limits the profibrotic cascade that typically leads to end-stage renal disease.[12, 33]

### 7.2 Restoration of Renal Antioxidant Enzymes

The kidney's vulnerability to hyperglycemia is largely due to the generation of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), which overwhelm the organ's endogenous antioxidant defenses.[13] *Phaleria macrocarpa* mitigates this by enhancing the activity of critical antioxidant enzymes:

1. **Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), Catalase (CAT), and Glutathione Peroxidase (GPx):** Diabetic rats treated with *P. macrocarpa* fractions for two weeks showed a significant restoration of these enzymes.[13]

2. **Malondialdehyde (MDA) Reduction:** MDA, a marker of lipid peroxidation, is elevated in the diabetic kidney. Treatment with *P. macrocarpa* suppressed MDA levels, thereby protecting the integrity of renal cell membranes.[13, 37]

3. **Filtration Markers:** The plant extracts have been proven to reduce renal hypertrophy and significantly lower Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine levels.[12, 13]

## 9. Toxicological Safeguards and Safety Profiles

### 9.1 Seed and Pit Toxicity

The seeds and pits are the most toxic parts of the plant, reported to contain phorboesters and other potential irritants that can cause severe poisoning if consumed raw.[1, 2, 41] In traditional usage, the fruit is always cooked, boiled, or processed into a dried form (simplicia).[5, 11]

### 9.2 Long-term and Subacute Safety

Subacute toxicity evaluations in mice have shown that the **500 mg/kg dose is safe** and provides a therapeutic effect without significant adverse histological changes.[38] However, extreme doses (above 1000 mg/kg) have been linked to congestion and structural changes in the liver, heart, and lungs.[38]

---

## 10. Conclusions and Clinical Outlook

*Phaleria macrocarpa* represents a sophisticated botanical repository of metabolites with profound implications for the treatment of diabetes mellitus and the prevention of its most debilitating renal complications.[6, 12, 33]

The synergy of its 25 key compounds—spanning the iron-chelating Mangiferin, the profibrotic-inhibitor Phalerin, the enzyme-blocking Swertianin, and the vascular-stabilizing Icariside C3—addresses the metabolic syndrome from multiple directions.[1, 10, 13, 21, 33]

Future clinical research should focus on validating these mechanisms in long-term human trials and exploring the pharmacokinetic interactions of these benzophenone and xanthone glycosides within the human metabolic environment.[34]
